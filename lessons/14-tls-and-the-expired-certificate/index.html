TLS and the certificate that expired at 3am

TLS gives you two things: encryption (nobody can read the traffic) and identity (you're really talking to example.com, proven by a certificate chain). Certificates expire by design — and the expired-cert outage is the incident almost every company has had, because nothing feels broken until the exact second it is.

~9 min readIntermediateHandshake · chain of trust · expiry

The handshake in plain terms

Every HTTPS connection starts with a TLS handshake, and it's shorter than its reputation suggests:

  • ClientHello — the client opens with the TLS versions and cipher suites it supports, plus SNI: the hostname it intends to talk to (that's how one IP can serve certs for many domains).
  • ServerHello + the certificate chain — the server picks the parameters and sends its certificates: the leaf cert for example.com → the intermediate CA that signed it → chaining up to a root CA that's already in the client's trust store (the client has the root; the server sends the rest).
  • The client verifies — three checks: is the signature chain valid all the way to a trusted root? does the hostname in the cert match the one I asked for? are the dates valid — notBefore ≤ now ≤ notAfter?
  • Key exchange — both sides derive symmetric session keys, and from there it's encrypted application data.

Those three checks are the identity half of TLS, and the third one is the sleeper. "Dates valid" is re-checked on every single new connection, by every client, with zero grace period. Not "roughly around expiry", not "with a warning window" — the second notAfter passes, the check flips from pass to fail everywhere at once.

03:00:00 — the second everything stopped

The setup is always mundane. The cert was issued 90 days ago. Renewal was manual — a calendar reminder someone snoozed — or automated by a cron job that broke silently months back and nobody noticed, because the cert it failed to renew was still valid at the time. Then notAfter arrives.

At that instant, every new handshake fails, across every client, simultaneously. Browsers throw ERR_CERT_DATE_INVALID full-screen. Mobile apps surface SSL exceptions their error handling never mapped to a friendly message. Other companies' backends calling your API start failing too — their TLS libraries run the exact same date check yours does. It's a 100% outage with zero crashed processes: the app is running, the load balancer is healthy, CPU is idle.

Two cruel details make it worse. Existing connections keep working briefly — the date check happens at handshake time, so long-lived keep-alive connections and already-established sessions carry on, which means some dashboards look half-alive and the outage seems "intermittent" while you're triaging. And it always lands at a terrible hour, because expiry timestamps are set by the issuance time, not by anyone's business hours. A cert issued at 3:00pm your time on a Tuesday might die at 3am on a Saturday, and the deadline does not negotiate.

Check any cert right now

You don't need a browser padlock menu — openssl shows you exactly what clients see, including the field that ends careers of cron jobs:

$ echo | openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com 2>/dev/null \
    | openssl x509 -noout -dates -issuer -subject
notBefore=Apr 26 03:00:00 2026 GMT
notAfter=Jul 25 03:00:00 2026 GMT    # ← the timestamp that decides when your night ends
issuer=C=US, O=Let's Encrypt, CN=R11   # the intermediate that signed your leaf
subject=CN=example.com                 # must match the hostname the client asked for

Renewal should be a machine's job. With certbot, the package installs a systemd timer (or cron entry) that runs twice a day and renews anything within 30 days of expiry — and the dry run lets you test the whole path without touching the real cert:

# test the entire renewal path — DNS, challenges, permissions — without renewing anything
$ certbot renew --dry-run

# confirm the timer actually exists and is scheduled (this is the cron job that "silently broke")
$ systemctl list-timers | grep certbot

# renewing writes the new cert to disk — the deploy hook makes nginx actually serve it
$ certbot renew --deploy-hook "systemctl reload nginx"

And because automation can break silently, monitor the thing the automation is supposed to guarantee — days remaining — not the automation itself:

# exit non-zero (→ alert) when fewer than 21 days remain
$ echo | openssl s_client -connect api.example.com:443 -servername api.example.com 2>/dev/null \
    | openssl x509 -noout -checkend $((21*24*3600)) \
    || echo "ALERT: cert on api.example.com expires in under 21 days"

If auto-renew works, this alert never fires. If auto-renew broke in March, this alert fires in June — three weeks before the 3am pager would have.

The chain of trust, in one paragraph. Your leaf cert isn't signed by a root CA directly — it's signed by an intermediate, which is signed by the root. Clients only ship with roots in their trust store, so the server must send the leaf and the intermediate, or the client can't build the path to a root it trusts. Forget the intermediate and you get the classic confusing incident: it works in Chrome (which caches intermediates it has seen elsewhere, or fetches them on the fly) but fails in curl, on Android, and in your partners' backends, which verify strictly with only what you sent. Always deploy the full chain file (fullchain.pem, not cert.pem) — and verify the whole chain, not just the leaf.
Test the failure path on purpose — the same discipline as Lesson 09's crash drills. Run certbot renew --dry-run before you need it, not during the incident. And know this trap: "SSL problems" right after a renewal often means the reload hook didn't fire — the new cert is sitting on disk while nginx keeps serving the old one from memory. openssl s_client against the live port tells you what's actually being served; openssl x509 -in fullchain.pem only tells you what's on disk. When they disagree, reload.

The takeaway

Certificate expiry is not an edge case — it's a scheduled outage with a timestamp you can read months in advance, and you defuse it with automation plus monitoring: a renewal timer that fires with 30 days to spare, a deploy hook that reloads the server, and an independent check that alerts when fewer than 21 days remain. Verify the whole chain, not just the leaf, or you'll ship the "works in Chrome, fails everywhere strict" special. And it's the same theme as every lesson before this one: recovery must be a mechanism, not a person — because the deadline will land at 3am, and the mechanism doesn't have to wake up.
